# The Lamp-Keeper's Count

Aldric went into the Cave of Skeldur alone, because there was no one left to send.

The Order of the Kindled Lamp had kept the Ruby of Calamir for four hundred years: a stone cut in the Elder Days by smiths who had seen the light before the Sun, and set in the shrine at Harrowmere so that no night in that country was ever wholly dark. Then Skeldur, who had once been numbered among the Order's wise before he learned to prefer being feared, came by night and took it. The lamp went out. The Order followed it, one grey head at a time, until nothing remained but a middle-aged priest with a mace he had never swung and a voice that carried well in a chapel and badly everywhere else.

He lit his lantern at the cave mouth, said the old words for a dead lamp, and went in.

---

In the first chamber a woman sat with her chin on her knees, watching the dark as though it owed her money. She looked at the mace, then at his face, and made up her mind about him in the space of a breath.

"Wynne," she said. "I came in after my brother. I'd rather not go out with nothing to show for it."

"Aldric. I've nothing to show for it either. I was hoping to find some."

It was the best bargain he struck in the whole cave, and he had not had to argue for it.

---

On the second level, on a plinth in a room floored with black glass, lay a crystal shaped like an eye and a flute of bone-white reed no longer than a hand. The crystal was the Eye of God, which saw through every glamour and every lie.

"The Eye," Wynne said. "Obviously the Eye."

Aldric stood a long while. He wanted it badly, and it took him some time to be honest about why: he wanted to be *sure* of people. He wanted never again to come into the chapel and find another bench empty. It was the wanting that decided him. He took the flute, left the Eye shining at nothing, and Wynne looked at him as though he had announced he was walking home.

---

On the third level they entered a chamber where a Dragon slept across its own gold, and the floor, which had been laid by someone who disliked visitors, gave way.

They fell a long time. What Aldric remembered afterwards was the Dragon's breathing, vast and patient, going by overhead like weather, and that it never once changed its rhythm. They landed on the fourth level in a heap of lantern-light and bruises, in a chamber that was already occupied.

A Hero and a woman, armed, and not interested in his greeting. The Hero struck first. For a long moment Aldric's mace and Wynne's borrowed knife held against the Hero's sword and nothing gave; then the Hero's guard broke, and the woman's after it, and when it was over neither would rise.

Aldric sat down against the wall. He had not known he could do that. He did not care for what it told him.

Wynne gathered the silver from the dead without ceremony and tipped it into his palm. "Something to show for it," she said. She was not unkind about it. He found that was worse.

---

The next chamber was cold, and the cave whispered in it.

It was not words, only their shape, a murmuring in the stone at the edge of hearing, and it said to anyone who stopped to listen: *Why follow a man who cannot fight?* A Wizard, an Ogre and a Giant stood about in the gloom like men with nothing to do and eternity to do it in. And Wynne, who had been the first to trust him, who had put silver in his hand, walked across the floor and stood among them. She set down her pack. She did not look at him.

Aldric did not draw the mace. He stood in the doorway with his lantern raised and said, in the voice that carried so well in a chapel, the first blessing of the Order, which begins *Here is a light, and you are welcome to it.* He had no idea what else to do.

The whisper faltered, like a voice losing its place. The Wizard laughed, a man caught out. The Ogre shrugged. The Giant stooped, picked up the pack Wynne had dropped, and held it out to her. And all four of them came back across the floor, Wynne last, and no one ever spoke of it.

---

After that they came more easily. A warrior-maid and a Man with a bad leg and a good laugh joined them in a chamber where a Magic Sword and a Magic Staff lay about like firewood. Aldric took the Sword, because someone had to and he alone would be embarrassed by it. The Staff went to the Wizard, who weighed it like a man hearing a familiar voice.

Then, in a chamber that glittered with gold and silver, came the Ghouls.

They came out of the walls without a sound. The Giant broke two, the Ogre one; the Wizard burned another into the ceiling; the Man and the warrior-maid held the door. Everyone held. Everyone but one.

Wynne did not cry out. Aldric reached her too late to be of any use. Then, because there was nothing else to do, he took the knife from her belt and put it in his own. He remembered what she had said in the first chamber, about something to show for it, and he did not say her name.

---

The Viper Pit was only a pit, and the vipers only vipers, but there were hundreds of them and the way across was a ledge two feet wide. The Giant looked at the pit. The Ogre looked at the Giant. Everyone looked at Aldric.

He took the flute out of his sleeve. It knew three notes, and they were the least impressive three notes he had ever heard, thin and sweet, like a child's whistle in an empty house. The vipers lay down one by one like sheaves, and the party crossed on tiptoe, seven of them, and not one adder lifted its head.

Halfway over it occurred to him that the Eye would have told him the vipers were there. It would not have put them to sleep.

---

Beyond the pit, Skeldur sat beside a rolled carpet with a Wizard on one side and an Ogre on the other, counting something on a tablet. He was not what Aldric had feared: only a tired old man in a good coat, looking up.

They fell on him before he could finish rising, the Giant, the Ogre, Aldric and both Wizards, while the warrior-maid and the Man dealt with his two attendants. It was not a fair fight and was not meant to be. Skeldur had time to lift a hand, and in the hand a light, and the light guttered and went out. The whisper in the walls, which had followed them across the fourth level, stopped as though a hand had been laid across its mouth.

Aldric stood over him and felt nothing he had a name for. There was a Carpet, which had evidently been how Skeldur travelled. It carried them up the stair like a bird and set them down on the third level without a sound.

---

At the head of the next stair stood a Giant, a Dwarf and a Man, who paid no heed to the blessing the first time. Aldric had been taught that you offer it twice. The second time, grudgingly, they came along.

On the second level they came to a hall where a Spectre stood vigil over a Unicorn. It lifted its head as they entered and looked along the file of faces. Aldric thought he knew who it was looking for, and perhaps that was only himself looking. It passed over the warrior-maid as it passed over all of them, and dropped its head again. It was not hostile. It was not glad. It was indifferent, the way grief is, and they went out past it very quietly, and no one spoke for the length of a stair.

---

They crossed a Deep Pool, the Giants ferrying the heavy things over on their shoulders, and on the far side stood the statue.

It was the height of two men, and in its brow was the Ruby of Calamir. Aldric had pictured it hanging in a sorcerer's hall. He had not pictured it being *held*.

"It will not fight," the Wizard said. "It will only not let go."

He leaned his Staff against the wall and took the statue by its stone shoulders, and the statue took hold of him. It was not what anyone would have called a battle. Two shapes swayed together in the lantern-light like old men refusing to be parted at a station. The Wizard had been told all his life that he was not strong. For one long minute the difference between them could have been written on a thumbnail.

He won by the width of a breath.

The Ruby came out of the stone warm in his hand, like something alive, and the cave, for an instant, remembered what light had been before the Sun.

---

On the first level a Ring and a Talisman lay unguarded, and Aldric took them both. And then there was Medusa. The Wizard raised the Staff on her threshold as a man raises a shield against a thrown spear, and her gaze, which had emptied better parties than theirs, glanced off it and went dull. Nobody turned to stone.

Then there was a door, and grey light beneath it.

He went out first, as was proper, and stood to one side in the wet grass and counted them as they came: the Wizard with the Ruby in both hands, the Ogre, the Giants, the Man with the bad leg, the warrior-maid, the rest. Fourteen, himself among them. Fourteen, who had come out of the dark because he had asked them. He counted again, and the second time he counted one more than were there.

He had gone in alone because there was no one left to send. It had not occurred to him that you could ask.

The lantern he had lit at the cave mouth was burning low. He held it out, and the Wizard touched the Ruby to the wick, and Aldric said the old words for a lamp; and this time they were not the words for a dead one.
