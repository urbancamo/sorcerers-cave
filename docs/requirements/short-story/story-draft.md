# The Lamp-Keeper's Count

*First draft. See `plan.md` for the game run it follows and `editor-notes.md` for the review.*

---

Aldric went into the Cave of Skeldur alone, because there was no one left to send.

The Order of the Kindled Lamp had kept the Ruby of Calamir for four hundred years: a stone cut in the Elder Days by smiths who had seen the light before the Sun, and set in the shrine at Harrowmere so that no night in that country was ever wholly dark. Then Skeldur, who had been numbered among the Order's own wise before he learned to prefer being feared, came by night and took it. The lamp went out. In the winter after, the Order went out with it, one grey head at a time, until all that remained was a middle-aged priest with a mace he had never swung and a voice that carried well in a chapel and badly everywhere else.

He lit his lantern at the cave mouth, said the old words for a dead lamp, and went in.

In the first chamber a woman sat with her chin on her knees, watching the dark as though it owed her money. She looked at the mace, then at his face, and made up her mind about him in the space of a breath.

"Wynne," she said. "I came in after my brother. I'd rather not go out without something to show for it."

"Aldric," he said. "I have nothing to show for it either. I'm hoping to find some."

It was, he thought afterwards, the best bargain he made in the whole cave, and he had not even had to argue.

---

On the second level, in a room with a floor of black glass, two things lay on a plinth: a pale crystal shaped like an eye, which the old writings called the Eye of God and which was said to see through every glamour and every lie, and a flute of bone-white reed, no longer than a hand.

"The Eye," Wynne said at once. "Obviously the Eye."

Aldric turned it over in his mind for longer than was comfortable. A thing that saw through every lie was a fine thing to carry, and a heavy one; the Order taught that you should take only what you could carry through a narrow door. And he had noticed, standing there, that he wanted the Eye very much, and that it was not for the reason he would have admitted to.

He took the flute. He left the Eye on its plinth, shining at nothing. Wynne looked at him as though he had told her he intended to walk home.

---

On the third level they stepped into a chamber where a Dragon lay asleep across its own gold, and the floor, which had been laid by someone who hated visitors, gave way.

They fell a very long way. Aldric remembered afterwards the *sound* of it, the rush, and above it the vast, patient breathing of the thing they were falling past, which did not wake. They landed on the fourth level in a heap of lantern-light and bruises, and found that the chamber they had landed in was already occupied.

A Hero and a woman, armed and unfriendly. There was no parley. The Hero struck first, and there was a moment, a long one, when Aldric's mace and Wynne's borrowed knife met the Hero's sword and neither side gave way. Then the Hero's guard broke, and the woman's after it, and when it was over both were on the floor and would not get up again.

Aldric sat down against the wall. He had not known that he could do that. He knew now, and he did not care for what it told him.

"Eat something," Wynne said, not unkindly, and put silver in his hand.

---

The next chamber was cold, and the cave whispered in it.

It was not words. It was the shape of words, a murmuring in the stone at the edge of hearing, and it said, to anyone who stopped to listen, *why are you following a man who can't fight?* In the chamber stood a Wizard, an Ogre and a Giant, lounging about like men with nothing to do and the whole of eternity to do it in. And Wynne, who had been brave since the first room, who had put silver in his hand, walked across the floor and stood among them. She dropped the pack she had carried so far. She did not look at him.

Aldric did not draw the mace. He had no idea what he did instead. He stood in the doorway with his lantern, and he said, in the voice that carried so well in a chapel, the first blessing of the Order, the one that begins *Here is a light, and you are welcome to it.* He said it twice. He had been taught that you offered it twice.

It was the best throw of his life, though no one threw anything; it was only that the thing in the room turned. The Wizard laughed, like a man caught out. The Ogre shrugged. The Giant bent to pick up the pack that Wynne had dropped. And all four of them came back across the floor, Wynne last, and none of them said a word about it, then or ever.

---

After that, they came more easily. A Hero's daughter, a Man with a bad leg and a good laugh, a second Wizard, a Giant who needed asking twice. In one chamber there was a Magic Sword and a Magic Staff lying beside two strangers who had no idea what to do with them. Aldric gave the Sword to himself, because someone had to carry it and he was the only one who would be embarrassed by it, and the Staff to the first Wizard, who weighed it in his hand like a man hearing a familiar voice.

On the fourth level, in a chamber of gold, there were Ghouls.

They came out of the walls like a bad idea. The Giant broke two; the Ogre, one; the Wizard burned a fourth into the ceiling. Everyone held. Everyone except one.

Wynne did not make a sound. She had been brave since the first chamber, and she was brave once too often.

Aldric did not say her name aloud, then. He thought of the first words she had said to him: *I'd rather not go out without something to show for it*. He picked up her knife, and put it in his belt, and they went on.

---

The Viper Pit was only a pit, and the vipers only vipers, but there were four hundred of them, and the only way across was a ledge as wide as a man's two feet. The Giant looked at the pit. The Ogre looked at the Giant. Everyone looked at Aldric.

He took the flute out of his sleeve.

It played three notes, which was all it knew. They were the least impressive three notes he had ever heard, thin and sweet, like a child's whistle in an empty house. The vipers lay down one by one, like sheaves, and the party crossed on tiptoe, thirteen of them, and not one adder lifted a head.

*Take only what you can carry through a narrow door.* It occurred to him, crossing, that the Eye would not have fitted on that ledge.

---

Skeldur was in the next room, and he was not what Aldric had feared. He was a tired old man in a good coat, sitting beside a rolled carpet with a Wizard of his own on one side and an Ogre on the other, counting something on a tablet. He looked up, and he knew Aldric at once. The look was the look of a man who has been robbed of a particular moment, one he had spent years rehearsing.

They went in before the Sorcerer had finished rising. It was not a fair fight and it was not meant to be: the Giant, the Ogre, the Priest, both Wizards, all on a single foe. Skeldur had time to raise one hand, and in the hand was a light, and the light was only a light, and it went out.

Aldric stood over him for a moment, and felt nothing he had a name for.

There was a Carpet. It had been, evidently, how Skeldur travelled; it took them up the stair like a bird, and set them down on the third level without a sound.

---

They passed a hall in which a Spectre stood vigil over a Unicorn.

It was the whitest thing Aldric had ever seen. It lifted its head as they came in and looked along the file of faces. It was looking for someone, and he knew, with a drop in his stomach, who it was looking for. The Unicorn found nothing in the faces that it wanted, and dropped its head again. It wasn't hostile. It wasn't glad. It was only indifferent, the way grief is indifferent, and they walked out past it very quietly, and no one spoke for the length of a stair.

---

They crossed a Deep Pool, the Giants carrying the heavy things two and three at a time, and on the far side was the statue.

It was the height of two men, and set in its forehead was the Ruby of Calamir. After four hundred years Aldric had half-expected it to be smaller.

"It will not fight," the Wizard said. "It will only not let go."

So the Wizard went and took hold of it, and what followed was nothing Aldric would have called a battle. A tired man in a long coat gripped the stone shoulders of a statue and the statue gripped back, and they swayed together in the dim like two drunkards. The Wizard had been told his whole life that he was not strong. For one long minute, the margin between them could have been written on a thumbnail.

The Wizard won by a single point.

The Ruby came out of the stone warm in his hand, like something alive, and the whole cave, for an instant, remembered what the dark had been before the Sun.

---

There was The Ring on the first level, and a Talisman, both of which Aldric put on without thinking. And there was Medusa.

The Wizard raised the Staff at the doorway of her chamber as one might raise a shield against a thrown spear, and her gaze, which had killed better parties than theirs, glanced off it and went dull. Nobody turned to stone. The Giant said something under his breath about the price of a good staff.

Then there was a door, and a grey light under it, and the whole party standing before it with their treasure in their arms.

Aldric went out first, as was proper, and then stood to one side in the wet grass and counted them as they came.

Fourteen. Himself, and the Wizard with the Ruby, and the Ogre and the Giants and the Man with the bad leg, and the rest. Fourteen, who had come out of the dark by being asked.

He counted them twice. The second time, he found that he was counting one more than were there.

He had gone in alone because there was no one left to send. He had not been right about that; there was no one left to *send*, but there were always people to *ask*. The Sorcerer had known how to take a thing. It had taken the Order four hundred years to learn how to be given one.

He took a lantern out of his pack, lit it from the Ruby, and said the old words for a lamp, and this time they were not the words for a dead one.
