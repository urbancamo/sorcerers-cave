I The Quest

Lead a band of adventurers down through a cave of many levels, gather what treasure you can, and climb back to the surface alive.

The cave is built one card at a time as you explore. Behind every doorway may lie an empty tunnel, a chamber heavy with gold, a sleeping dragon, or a trap that drops you into the dark below. Nothing is fixed until you turn it over.

Your score is the worth of everyone who walks back out into the daylight, plus the treasure they carry. A party wiped out underground scores nothing — so the real art is knowing when to press deeper and when to turn for home. This manual describes the solitaire game first (multiplayer has its own section): your progress is saved automatically on this device.

Your game code. Every game is stamped with a unique four-letter code, shown at the top of the screen in the CODE box. Jot it down: entering it under Resume a game on the title screen drops you straight back into the exact same game, right where you left off. Your progress is saved after every move, so you do not need to press Save & exit first — the code works at any time, even mid-turn.

II Building Your Party

Spend up to 6 selection points recruiting your expedition before you enter.

Each creature costs points to recruit and is worth points if it survives the cave. Stronger, more valuable creatures cost more, so a typical party is a careful mix — muscle to fight, backs to carry treasure, and a specialist or two. You may take up to twelve members, but the 6-point budget is the real limit.

III Exploring the Cave

Move through glowing doorways and stairways; each new step turns over the next area.

Moving

  • Click a glowing doorway marker, or press N / E / S / W (or the arrow keys — ↑ is North, → East, ↓ South, ← West), to step that way.
  • Press U / D (or click the corner stair markers) to climb or descend a stairway.
  • Drag to orbit, scroll to zoom. Snap to current tile gives a clean overhead view; the Levels buttons focus a single floor; Free orbit shows the whole cave.

A doorway only leads somewhere if its edge matches the area beyond it. If it doesn't, you've found a dead end — that exit closes, but you may try another. The card you drew is shown face up for a few seconds so you can read it, then it fades to a grey fog card with a stub on each side you have tried it from (a second party, or the same one coming at it from another way, adds its own stub), and stays that way, as at the table: from then on you must remember what it was (until a party enters it from another side, when the fog fades to the real card, or the Sibyl shows it to you). (If a dead end would leave your party with nowhere left to go, the optional Dead End rule may step in — see House Rules.) When you descend, the floor you came from is hidden automatically so it never blocks your view of where you now stand.

IV Special Areas

Five named areas behave unlike ordinary tunnels and chambers.

V Hazards

When a chamber turns up a hazard, it strikes immediately — before any treasure or strangers are dealt with.

If several appear at once they resolve in this order: Earthquake → Medusa → Ghouls → Mutiny → Trap.

Earthquake

The area you just came from collapses. It is removed from play — drawn as rubble, impassable, and the doorway back to it is sealed. You cannot return the way you came. The earthquake card is left lying on the rubble to mark what fell.

Medusa

Her gaze sweeps the party; a die is rolled for each member, and a 1 or 2 turns that member to stone (the rolls are shown one per member). A petrified member is marked with a stone badge and can do nothing until freed by the Magic Staff (a wizard bearing the staff is immune). Medusa lurks for good: her card stays on the chamber floor, and she rolls against the party again every time you re-enter. If her gaze petrifies your whole party, the expedition ends there.

Ghouls

House rule Where the revised combat is on, the ghouls are instead a lasting hazard that fights one round only: one strength-2 ghoul for each creature in your party, each matched one-on-one against one of your creatures, with no surprise on either side. Everything is rolled once; a creature that loses its duel is slain, and any ghoul left standing then withdraws and the combat is over. There is no retreating from them — they are a hazard — and the ghouls stay in the chamber to attack the next party. They are met after the chamber's other hazards, except a Trap, which is always dealt with last: if the chamber also holds a Trap, you fight the ghouls first and only then fall (or, with a Dwarf's guidance, make your choice). The chamber's other strangers wait until the ghouls are done. The Talisman still wards them off. Their arrival is announced before anything is rolled, and your creatures are matched against them for you.

Each member fights a round against the ghouls (strength 2) at full fighting strength — a Magic Sword or Strength Potion counts — and anyone who loses is slain (the rolls are shown per member; no surprise on either side). The attack forces the party to drop all heavy treasure onto the floor, which you may reclaim once the fight is over. Like Medusa, ghouls lurk: their card stays in the chamber and they attack again whenever you re-enter. The Talisman wards them off entirely.

Mutiny

Your befriended allies desert — reverting to strangers and dropping any treasure they carried back onto the floor. If your party is made up entirely of allies, one loyal soul remains. You then face the deserters as you would any group of strangers. The Mutiny card is left lying on the tile where it was drawn, whether or not anyone deserted.

Trap

The floor gives way and the party plunges to the area directly below — and there is no climbing back. A Dwarf guides the party safely past a trap. (Where the standing-traps rule is on, each Dwarf guides one trap — see House Rules.)

No way back A trap fall is one-way. You cannot withdraw or retreat back up, so plan to find another route out — or a secret stairway down and around.

House rule Where standing traps are switched on, a trap stays in its chamber and a Dwarf's guidance becomes a choice — you may even fall on purpose, and arrive with the advantage of surprise. See House Rules.

VI Strangers & Reactions

When a chamber holds creatures, you must decide how to meet them before touching any treasure there.

You have three choices, taken in the same turn:

  • Withdraw — back out the way you came, leaving the strangers and any treasure behind.
  • Attack — fight at once (see Fights). Striking the instant you enter a fresh chamber gains you the advantage of surprise.
  • Test their reaction — approach and roll to see how they feel about you.

The leader, and the reaction roll

One stranger leads the group, by this order of priority:

Spectre › Dragon › Wizard › Hero / Woman Hero › Priest › Man / Woman › Giant › Ogre › Troll › Dwarf

Roll a die for the leader; the rest react the same way. Charisma adds +1 and each curse subtracts 1 (curses stop biting once the Sorcerer is dead) — but a natural 1 is always hostile, no matter the bonuses. The result is one of:

  • Friendly — they join your party as allies, and the chamber's treasure is yours.
  • Indifferent — they ignore you for now. Next turn you may test again, attack, or leave. Test to indifferent three times and they become permanently indifferent to your party: you may then walk on out by any exit, though the treasure stays guarded (you can't take it), and they stay indifferent if you return.
  • Hostile — they attack at once, and a round is fought (with the strangers holding the advantage of surprise).
Who reacts how Gentle folk are easily befriended; a Dragon or Spectre almost never is. Bringing a charismatic leader, and avoiding curses, swings the odds your way.

The Unicorn

A single Unicorn lies somewhere in the deck. It has fighting strength 4 and no magic, carries nothing at all, and is worth 4 points if it walks out of the cave with you. You cannot recruit it at the party screen — you can only meet it.

Its heart is never in doubt: tested on its own, a Unicorn is always friendly, and the die cannot change that. It ranks lowest of all the creatures, though, so it only leads a group when it stands alone; in company it takes its cue from the group's leader, like any other member.

  • It needs a Woman. A Unicorn joins only a party with a living Woman or Woman Hero (the party table marks them Befriends the Unicorn).
  • Without one it stays to guard. The reaction still reads “Friendly — but it stays to guard the chamber.” Your party moves straight on, and the chamber is quiet for you from then on, as if its occupants had become permanently indifferent. The treasure it guards stays out of reach — even a Thief cannot lift it — unless you choose to Attack the guardians.
  • It leaves with the last Woman. An allied Unicorn stays only while your party holds a living Woman or Woman Hero. When the last one falls in a fight, the Unicorn departs and leaves the party.
  • As an ally it fights in the front line at strength 4 (it is no caster and cannot hang back to lend magic), and it cannot carry treasure.

VII Fights

A fight runs one round per turn until one side is wiped out, put to sleep, or your party retreats.

Surprise

The side with surprise adds +1 to all its rolls in the first round. Your party gains it by attacking immediately on entering a chamber by a fresh doorway (or by magic carpet). You do not get it if you stopped to test the strangers first, or if you fell in through a trap — unless the fall was deliberate: where the optional standing-traps rule is on, a party that drops through a trap on purpose arrives with surprise (see House Rules). Strangers gain surprise when they turn hostile on being approached.

House rule Where the revised combat is switched on, a fight is played quite differently — round by round on a formation screen, with a fresh set of choices each round. See House Rules; the rest of this section describes the ordinary fight.

Pairing off

Each fighter is matched against a stranger and a die is rolled for each side; total strength plus the die decides the winner, and the loser is slain. A tie kills no one.

  • If you outnumber the strangers, two of your members may gang up on one foe.
  • If they outnumber you, a single member can be set against at most two foes at once; any further strangers stand idle that round, and you face the strongest combination they can form.
  • Priests and wizards may fight hand-to-hand or hang back, adding their magical power to a front-line fighter. Any number of casters can pool their power against one enemy.
  • Enemy priests, wizards and the Sorcerer do likewise: when you can't engage them all hand-to-hand, the leftover enemy casters lend their magical power to the foes you are fighting. They're shown behind the front foes, marked “lends magic”, so the enemy strength always reflects who is really in the fight.

Strangers come armed

Fighting artefacts lying among a chamber's treasure are not safe from the creatures guarding it. When a fight begins, the strangers pick up whatever they can use — the Magic Sword, the Magic Staff and The Ring (and, with the Extension Kit, the Magic Axe and Magic Shield) — according to who can wield what: the Sword goes first to a Hero, Woman Hero, Man or Woman; the Axe to a Dwarf; the Staff to a Wizard, Priest or other caster; the Shield to those already armed; The Ring to the strongest creature that may wear it.

  • What a stranger bears counts: a Sword or Axe adds to its fighting strength, a Staff to its magic, and a stranger's Shield cancels the magic of whoever is matched against it. A stranger wearing The Ring adds +1 to every stranger's die. The fight panel lists each as a modifier — “Magic Sword · Hero”, and so on.
  • Kill a bearer and its gear drops to the floor; a surviving stranger may snatch it up for the next round. Win the fight and anything dropped is yours to loot.
  • If you retreat, every artefact the strangers carried is returned to the floor before you go.
  • A Ring-bearer on level 4 or deeper is invincible, just as your own would be — but if it still loses its match it vanishes with the Ring, leaving its other gear behind.
  • The Eye of God switches all of this off — see Treasure & Artefacts.

Choosing who falls

When two of your members lose a fight together, you decide which one is lost. State your preference and roll: on a 4, 5 or 6 you get your wish; otherwise the other falls. (The Ring protects its bearer — see below.)

Retreat

After at least one round has been fought, you may retreat by any of the tile's doorways or stairways — even one you haven't explored yet (a tile is drawn for it). The strangers and any treasure dropped in the fight are left behind. But if that way turns out to be a dead end, the party can't escape it and must fight another round this turn — no further retreat is offered until that round is fought (then you may try a different exit). You cannot retreat before the first round, nor back up a trap you fell through.

They remember Strangers you retreat from stay hostile to your party for the rest of the game — return to that chamber and they attack on sight, with no chance to test them. (Other parties may still approach them.) The one exception: where standing traps are on, a party that comes back by deliberately dropping through a trap catches them off guard, and it is your party that has surprise.

Two foes to respect

The Dragon (fighting strength 6) is the deadliest common foe — but slay one single-handed and that hero's worth is doubled at scoring forever. A Charmed Flute lulls dragons to sleep so the party can walk straight past them.

The Spectre cannot be harmed by ordinary blows. Without magic or the Magic Sword in hand, it slays your strongest member each round. The Eye of God destroys spectres outright.

VIII The Sorcerer

The single most powerful dweller in the cave — best avoided, unless you come prepared.

A lone Sorcerer lurks somewhere in the deck. With fighting strength 4 and magical power 9, he fights at an effective strength of 13 — far beyond a Dragon — and as the highest-ranking creature he always leads any group he joins and turns it hostile. He is never befriended, and carries no point value of his own.

He is too mighty to be put to sleep or to have his magic wholly unmade: Lotus Dust and the Eye of God each reduce his Strength by only 2. Bring both and his 13 drops to 9 — still formidable, but no longer hopeless for a strong party. Without them, meeting the Sorcerer is very nearly a death sentence; withdraw if you still can.

If you can fell him Slaying the Sorcerer is the cave's crowning feat. His death is worth a +30 bounty at scoring, and — because a curse has no effect once the Sorcerer is dead — it lifts every curse on your party at a stroke: no more −1 on your rolls, and the −30 scoring penalty is erased. In the original boxed game he rules a great cavern on the fourth level, the Sorcerer's Den; here he is simply the deadliest creature you may chance upon — but the bounty for besting him stands.

House rule Where the Sorcerer's terms are on, a beaten Sorcerer need not die: you may spare him in return for a teleport to any area you have already discovered. Slaying him still earns the bounty and lifts every curse; sparing him earns neither. See House Rules.

IX Treasure & Artefacts

Unguarded treasure is yours to pick up; guarded treasure waits until the strangers are befriended or beaten.

Heavy treasure

Silver, Gold and Gems are heavy — they count against a member's carrying capacity. When you pick a piece up, only the members who have the spare capacity to bear it are offered (the party panel lets you move items between members or drop them on the floor). The Treasure Chest is heaviest of all at 100 kg, so only a strong back — a Giant, or an empty-handed Ogre — can carry it. A member must be carrying the chest to open it; do so and a die decides your fortune: a curse, a Spectre (which the Eye of God destroys on sight, and the Talisman drives off on level 4 or deeper — just like any other Spectre), worthless sand, or Silver, Gold, or Gems.

Artefacts

Artefacts weigh nothing and grant powers. Some are used up; others serve as long as you hold them.

X Secret Doors

More often than not, a stairway leads to an area where no matching stair is drawn. That hidden end is a secret door.

When you descend onto a chamber that shows no stair up — or climb onto one with no stair down — your party learns of a secret door there, and can use it to retrace their steps. Each one is marked on the floor with a lettered door card (A, B, C…), laid in the order you discover them, so you can read at a glance how the levels of the cave connect.

The glowing up- or down-stair marker still appears on that tile — step onto it to travel back through the secret door.

XI Leaving the Cave & Scoring

Climb any stairway up from level 1 to leave the cave — and bank your score.

Leaving is one-way: once your party steps out, the expedition is over. You'll be shown a roll call of who made it out and what each carried, with the total tallied. Enter a name to record it on the shared high-score board, which keeps separate tables for solitaire and multiplayer games, for base-game and Extension Kit games, and for games with or without the Sibyl — eight tables in all, because those games can't fairly be compared. Switch between them on the High Scores screen. You can also end a run at any time with Quit — but quitting abandons your party to the cave: they do not climb out. The score they had gathered is still tallied (a wiped party, of course, scores nothing).

How the score is reckoned

  • The points value of every member who walks out alive (stone and fallen members count for nothing).
  • Plus the points value of all treasure they carry.
  • Dragon-slayers who killed a dragon single-handed count double.
  • Plus any loot banked from the Treasure Chest, and +30 for slaying the Sorcerer.
  • Great feats — a single-handed Dragon kill, felling the Sorcerer, freeing the Lost Ruby, drinking the Elixir — are celebrated on screen as they happen.
  • −30 if the party is under a curse — a single flat penalty, however many curses you carry. Slaying the Sorcerer lifts every curse and erases this penalty.
All or nothing A party wiped out underground scores zero. Greed is the surest way to lose everything — know when to turn for the surface.

XII Multiplayer Games

Up to four parties race the same cave at once, taking turns from their own browsers — first to the richest haul out wins.

Creating and joining a game

  • Create a game from the title screen: choose your party name and a colour, and you become the host. The game is given a unique four-letter code.
  • Share that code with your friends. Each of them picks Join Multiplayer Game, enters the code in their own browser, and chooses a party name and an unused colour (colours already taken are greyed out).
  • Up to four players may gather in the lobby. When everyone's ready the host starts the game — after that no one new can join.

Game options

Before the game starts the host may switch on any of these in the lobby (everyone else sees them as read-only chips). They are fixed for the whole game once it begins.

  • Fog of war — tiles you have never stood on show only as face-down stubs, so other parties' discoveries stay hidden until you have been there yourself.
  • Concurrent exploration — no waiting for turns: every party explores freely at once. A fight still holds the parties taking part in it.
  • Extension kit — the kit's decks and recruits for everyone.
  • Sibyl — the Sibyl joins the shared pack. Each party gets its own single chance to test her.

Choosing parties

Everyone drafts their party from one shared pack of creatures, so the draft is taken in turns — a card another player takes is gone for the rest of you. Once the last player has chosen, the expedition begins.

Turn by turn

  • Players take turns in a set order. The “Now” marker in the top bar (in that party's colour) always shows whose turn it is; you can only act on your turn.
  • A turn plays out until your party is back at rest — a reaction, picking up treasure, and so on. A fight against strangers is the exception: it is fought one round per turn, so other parties act between your rounds.
  • The cave is shared: it grows as everyone explores, and you'll see the other parties as small coloured tokens on the map where they currently stand.

Keeping in touch

  • Toast messages slide in under the depth bar to announce what every other party does — defeating or befriending creatures, taking treasure, descending or climbing, and finishing their run.
  • A docked chat (bottom-left) lets the players talk; a red dot on the “Chat” tab marks unread messages, and incoming chat is also mirrored as a toast.

When a party finishes

A party's run ends when it escapes the cave, is wiped out, or the player abandons it (the multiplayer Quit → Abandon the expedition). The outcome and score are recorded to a separate multiplayer high-score table and announced to everyone still below. You are not kicked out — you land on the standings, and the whole game ends once every party has finished.

The standings page

A live scoreboard ranks every party by score, showing finished parties' outcomes and the live depth, turns, enemies slain and running score of those still exploring. From it you can:

  • Dip into the cave — drop into a free-roaming, read-only view to watch the action.
  • Follow a party — click any row to see exactly what that player sees, until you go back to the standings.
  • My run — review your own party's final score breakdown (then “Back to Standings”).
  • Leave to menu — your party stays in the game; you can rejoin later with the code. (Abandon, by contrast, ends your run for good.)

Meeting another party

Parties share one cave, so they can meet. When another party stands on the same tile as yours, an Also here strip names it and offers up to three buttons: Trade, Attack and Unite. Whoever you are dealing with answers from their own browser, so each step has a time limit — if it runs out, the step takes its default and play goes on.

Trading

Trade offers the other party an exchange. Each side picks what it gives — treasure, artefacts, even members — and both must confirm. The swap happens all at once or not at all: if anything has gone missing or would overload someone's carrying capacity, nothing moves and the confirmations are cleared. Either side may call it off. (An Eye of God passed on in a trade carries no curse.) Trade offers wait about a minute.

Fighting another party

Attack is greyed out, with the reason as its tooltip, when a fight would not be allowed: not across the Viper Pit, Deep Pool, Whirlpool or Chasm, not while strangers still stand in the chamber, and not while one of you is already fighting them.

  • Surprise. The attacker gets the usual +1 in the first round — unless it followed the defender in, so that both parties came in by the same way.
  • Heavy treasure is thrown down on the tile by both sides before the fight, as in any fight. The victor gathers the spoils from the floor.
  • The layout. The defender lines up first, with casters behind only if numbers allow. The attacker then engages — one against one, one against two or two against one, never two against two — and must engage every defender it can. Last, the defender assigns any background casters to the engagements, and the round is fought.
  • The round uses the same arithmetic as a fight against strangers. The strongest of each losing group falls, and the Ring protects its bearer as ever. The fallen's belongings are spilled for the eventual victor. A party reduced to nothing is out of the game.
  • Rounds alternate between the two parties' turns. Either side may retreat: it leaves its dropped treasure behind and gets two turns to get clear. Both sides may also agree to stop, and each takes back its own dropped treasure. A stage nobody answers within about 45 seconds takes its default rather than stalling the game.

Unions

Unite proposes that another party march with yours under your command; they have about a minute to accept. A united party joins its members into the commander's band: each non-commander forfeits one turn to join, and while united they take no turns of their own — the commander plays for the whole force, including in a fight against another party, where the strongest of the union must face the strongest of the enemy. A union ends when the commander chooses Dissolve or a member picks Leave union. Allies the union has won together are then divided by agreement: every former member must name the same new home for each ally, and a split vote leaves the ally neutral, behind where the union broke up. If a union fells the Sorcerer, the 30-point bounty is shared equally among its seats.

Secret doors Secret-door knowledge is personal in a shared cave: you may use a hidden stair only once your party has used it or watched a party standing with you use it — or if you hold the Charmed Flute.

XIII The Extension Kit

An optional expansion: six new special areas, four new hazards, five new recruits (plus two you'll only ever meet), and six new artefacts and treasures.

Every rule described so far assumes the base game's 60 area tiles and 71 small cards. Tick the Extension kit box on the party selection screen — solo, or as the host in a multiplayer lobby — before you set out, and the decks grow by 30 tiles and 30 small cards for that game only, drawing in everything below. Leave it unticked and none of this applies; every rule described elsewhere in this manual is unaffected.

New special areas

New hazards

With the kit on, the resolution order lengthens to: Earthquake → Spell → Harpies → Medusa → Ghouls → Quarrel → Desertion → Mutiny → Trap (the Trap is always last).

Desertion

Roll a die for every ally in the party — original party members never risk this one. A roll of 1 or 2 and that ally disappears for good, taking everything they carried, worn or wielded alike; nothing is left behind to loot. A Wolf ally never rolls at all. A clean sweep of safe rolls earns the party a quiet line of reassurance instead.

Harpies

If the party carries no artefacts at all, or holds the Talisman, they simply circle off and lurk — the same check runs again on your next visit. Otherwise every artefact any living member holds, worn or merely carried, is gone at once — swept away to the Lair, straight onto its floor if you've already found it, or held in reserve until you do. Losing the Eye of God this way still curses the party, exactly as losing it any other way would. A card that strikes is spent for good; one that only lurked comes back to try again.

Quarrel

The two members who'd hit hardest in an ordinary fight — by strength, magic, and artefacts combined — turn on each other for one round. Each rolls a die; the loser falls exactly as a stranger's blow would, revivable by the Healing Balm like any other casualty. The Wolf and the Lion never take part, however strong they are — and if no one else is left to fight, the card simply fizzles.

Spell

The last ordinary tunnel the party walked through — not a chamber, not the Gateway, never one already collapsed by an earthquake — is shuffled back into the large pack, and a fresh, sealed tile takes its place, to be discovered again as if for the first time. Any secret door it held vanishes with it. With nothing eligible behind you, or the large pack empty, the spell simply fizzles.

New creatures

Five new creatures join the selectable roster — the Witch, Scholar, Thief, Lion and Wolf — at the costs below. Turning the kit on also revises two familiar prices: the Ogre drops to cost 4 (was 5) and the Troll to cost 3 (was 4), and the kit's extra Woman and Dwarf cards raise each of their stocks from 3 to 4.

The Scholar and the Witch use artefacts exactly as a Priest does; the Thief plays a Man's part with the Charmed Flute. The Lion and the Wolf touch no artefact at all and carry nothing — pure, cheaply-priced muscle. The Wolf is the hardiest constitution in the kit: Medusa's gaze passes over it, Mutiny and Desertion's rolls don't touch it, and it's excused from ever standing in a Quarrel. The Thief, uniquely, can walk treasure out of a chamber the party has talked down to permanent indifference — no fight, no test, just a name on the card. In the leadership order that decides a whole group's reaction (see Strangers & Reactions), the Scholar and Witch rank beside the Priest, the Thief beside a Man or Woman, the Lion beside an Ogre, and the Wolf beside a Troll.

Two you'll never recruit

The Apprentice's loyalty is a bet on the Sorcerer's own life: while he lives, only a roll of six wins her reaction over, hostile the rest of the way — no indifferent middle ground at all. The moment he falls, that same six turns hostile too. Win that prize and she turns on the party in the same instant, wherever she's standing, taking nothing with her. She's worth nothing at the final count regardless — even a clean escape leaves her behind. House rule Under the revised combat she does not turn at once: see there.

The Demon never takes a seat among a chamber's ordinary strangers. Drawn from the small pack, it steps into whichever area the party has just left and waits there, springing the instant they return — no reaction test, just a fight. Only magic reaches it, and so, in any hand at all, does the Magic Axe; without either, it cuts down the party's strongest member before a blow is even considered. Win, and it collapses to ash rather than joining the usual tally of the fallen.

New heavy treasure

The Idol is fifty kilograms of stone — twice the burden of a haul of Silver or Gold — and does nothing at all along the way: no use, no passive gift. Only at the final reckoning does it speak: a die is cast for it there, once, and its worth is ten times whatever that die shows, from as little as 10 points to as much as 60. Carried out by the dead or the stone-turned, like any other loot, it's worth nothing.

Crypt / Gems doesn't drop onto the floor when drawn — it parks, remembered as a single spot in that chamber, and from then on, any turn you're at rest there, an Enter the crypt option is on offer. One d6 decides it for good: 1–2 is an inescapable trap — the whole party falls a level, and even a Dwarf's usual trap-guiding talent doesn't apply here. A 3–6 turns the parked card into the gems themselves: 25 kg, 20 points, an ordinary pickup from there. Either roll spends the crypt for good.

XIV The Sibyl

An optional seer who never fights — and who will show you what lies hidden.

Tick the Sibyl box on the party selection screen — solo, or as the host in a multiplayer lobby — and one extra card, the Sibyl, is shuffled into the small pack for that game. Leave it unticked and nothing in this section applies. Like the Extension Kit it changes the game's results, so scores for Sibyl games go in their own tables.

Meeting her

She is not one of a chamber's strangers. When she turns up she waits to one side, and you meet her only after the other strangers have been dealt with — befriended, beaten, or grown permanently indifferent to you. Then you may test her, once: all you can do is test her or withdraw — there is nothing to attack.

  • 5–6 — friendly. She joins you and stays with you, and is listed at the foot of the party roster (marked guide) for as long as she travels with you. She cannot fight, carry or be harmed.
  • 1–4 — indifferent. She stays in that chamber, and your party never gets a second chance to ask. (In a multiplayer game each party has its own single chance.)
  • She is never hostile. Charisma and curses adjust the roll as usual.
  • She does not guard treasure: loot found in her company is simply yours to take once she has spoken.

Travelling with her

The Sibyl is a companion, not a member of the party. She cannot fight or be harmed, cannot mutiny or desert, is worth no points, never counts as one of your living members (so she can never be the last one standing), and will not leave the cave — she is left behind when you climb out. The party panel notes that she is with you.

The look

While she is with you, a Sibyl row appears among your actions whenever the party is at rest. It costs nothing — no turn is used, and you may look as often as you like:

  • Look at the top of the pack — the next area card you would draw if you stepped into unexplored space.
  • Look at a face-down tile — the button is enabled whenever any dead-end card lies face down. Press it, then click one of the ringed face-down tiles on the map: its fog card fades to the real card for a few seconds, wrapped in the same soft violet glow as the top-of-the-pack card, and then fades back, so you must remember what you saw. Cards are normally examined only in the course of exploring; she lets you see them now.

The card appears over the view as a faint picture that fades in and out, and a line beneath the buttons spells out its exits and any special area. She shows area cards only — never the small cards (creatures, treasure and hazards) in the chamber pack.

Multiplayer Only you see what she shows. The other parties are told only that you consulted the Sibyl.

XV House Rules

Four optional rules, switched on or off for the whole game server rather than chosen by the player.

Unlike the Extension Kit and the Sibyl, you cannot tick these on the party screen: whoever runs the game decides whether they are in force, and a game keeps the rules it was started with. All four are for solitaire games only. If a rule isn't switched on, everything else in this manual applies unchanged.

The Dead End rule

Normally a card that fails to connect just closes that exit and you try another. It can, rarely, leave a party boxed in with nowhere left to go. With this rule on, the game checks before accepting a dead end:

  • It looks at every tile your party can still walk back to through doors that work. An untried doorway or stairway counts as a way on — a door onto empty space (while the pack lasts), or one that points at a tile already on the table but doesn't connect (trying it is free). Working doors you have already used, collapsed tiles, and a stairway up out of the cave do not count; neither does dropping through a trap or crossing a Whirlpool.
  • If any way on exists, the dead end stands as usual.
  • If none does, the card that made the dead end goes back into the pack at a random place and another is drawn for the same door, until a way opens or the cards run out. The same rescue applies to a card that does connect but would shut the party into a loop with no way out.
  • A failed retreat is never rescued — it still costs another round of the fight. If the rule finds no way on at all you are told “There are no viable exits left” and may play on, or quit.

A small badge on the map marks a card that was swapped.

The Sorcerer's terms

Beat the Sorcerer and, before anything else, you choose his fate:

  • Slay him — the usual: the +30 bounty, every curse lifted, any Apprentice turning on you.
  • Spare him — on condition that he teleports you, and the treasure in the chamber, to any area you have already discovered. A map of the cave lets you pick the destination, and glowing particles rise over the chamber you are leaving, the screen fades slowly to black with the Sorcerer's words, then fades up on your destination with the particles rising there too. There is no sudden flash. Sparing him forfeits the bounty and lifts no curse. He stays behind in the chamber you left.

You can choose any discovered area on any level, except one that has collapsed, that is not yet turned face-up, or that has no firm floor to land on (a Whirlpool, Deep Pool or Viper Pit). No turn is spent, but magic leaves no way back: you cannot withdraw from where you land. If the Eye of God is present he cannot be spared — he is slain.

Standing traps and the Dwarf's choice

Under the printed rules a trap “continues in effect”. With this rule on, it really does:

  • A drawn trap stays in its chamber after it fires or is avoided, and meets you again whenever you return.
  • When the traps in a chamber resolve for a party whose living Dwarves can cover every one of them — as they are drawn, or on a later entry — play pauses and asks: Guided past the trap, or Fall through the trap. Falling drops the whole party one level, one way, as any trap does, but it counts as deliberate.
  • A deliberate fall gains surprise (+1 in the first round, if you attack at once) on the chamber below — even one you have visited, and even one whose strangers you once retreated from, in which case it is your party that has surprise, not theirs. So does a party that knowingly walks back into a trap chamber without a Dwarf and falls again. A trap drawn on this very entry is a surprise to you, and earns none.
  • One Dwarf, one trap. Each living Dwarf can guide the party past one trap, so a party with n Dwarves can pass n traps in a chamber — two Dwarves cover two. With more traps than Dwarves the party falls; that is not a choice, so it earns no surprise.
  • If a Dwarf is lost — killed in a fight in the chamber, say — and the party is left with fewer Dwarves than there are traps, it is no longer guided: the first time it tries to leave (by a doorway, stairway, withdrawal or retreat) it falls, and that fall is an accident with no surprise. Leaving by Magic Carpet, by the Sorcerer's teleport or down the Chasm does not trigger it.
  • As ever, there is no withdrawing or retreating back up after any trap fall.

The revised combat

A fight is no longer one pairing and one roll. It is played as a series of rounds, and each round the game asks you for a few decisions in turn. Whoever started the fight is the attacker and stays so; the other side is the defender.

  1. The Scroll (attacker only, if you carry it) — see below.
  2. Defender: the front line. The defender puts some of its free creatures into the front line. If you defend, drag the creatures you want into the front-line box (or tap them), at least the number asked, and confirm.
  3. Attacker: pairings. The attacker matches each of its free creatures one-to-one against a creature in that front line, for as long as any can be paired. If you attack, tap one of your fighters and then the foe it will face; the Confirm Initial Pairings button wakes once every fighter that can be paired is. Confirming only sets the pairings; you may then send spare creatures into the matches before the round is rolled. A Spectre or Demon cannot be paired with a fighter who has no magic.
  4. Extra creatures. Either side may then send a spare creature into a match (two against two is allowed, never more) or a Priest or Wizard behind it to lend magic. If you are the side moving, drag a spare creature onto the match (a caster onto its “behind” slot), or tap the creature and then the match. You need not move anyone.
  5. A look before the dice. If the strangers paired up with your creatures or sent spare creatures into matches, and you have had no decision since, the game pauses once so you can see how they stand. You can only press Fight (or retreat) then; nothing can be moved.
  6. The matches are fought. A die is rolled for each side of each match. A match that cannot be decided — neither side can beat the other on any roll — simply stays as it is.

The Ring and curses add to or take from your dice as usual, and the first round carries the surprise bonus as before. A match you lose costs you its front line, with the same choice of who falls when two stood together. A match you win removes the foe. The fight ends when you have won, when your party is gone, or when no creature on either side can continue a match, be sent into one, or break a stalemate — and then you must retreat. Matches already in play carry over from round to round.

The rules that change with it:

  • The Scroll is no longer used up. If your party attacks, carries the Scroll with a human present, has no Eye of God, and has not used it in this area before, you are asked before each round: destroy every stranger without magical power (a Ring-bearer on the fourth level or deeper too: the Ring falls to the floor; the Sibyl is never harmed), or — in the first round only — take +1 to surprise instead. Either way the party is cursed, and the Scroll can't be read again in the same area.
  • The Treasure Chest's Spectre fights for one round only, with no surprise, and then vanishes. Anyone it kills stays dead.
  • The Ghouls fight one round only, one ghoul against each creature of yours (see Hazards).
  • The Apprentice no longer turns the instant the Sorcerer dies. She stays at your side while you decide: leave the area (by any route) and she is left behind, harmless, with whatever she carried on the floor; or stay (a button, with a confirmation) and she turns, and she and every stranger in the area attack you with surprise.

These are Peter's reformulated fighting rules, and parts are still provisional. A game played with the revised combat cannot be recorded on the leaderboard. The strangers' side is played by the same computer opponent as before.

A few hard-won tips

  • Bring a strong back (Ogre, Troll) — treasure left behind scores nothing.
  • A Woman or Woman Hero is the key to a Unicorn: without one it will only stand guard over the treasure.
  • A Dwarf is cheap insurance against a trap — and where standing traps are on, a way to drop down on purpose, with surprise. Charisma makes strangers into allies.
  • Leave a fight's best artefacts out of the strangers' reach: they pick up what lies in the chamber.
  • Attack the instant you enter for the surprise bonus — testing first throws it away.
  • Befriending strangers is often safer (and richer) than fighting them.
  • Watch your depth: the deeper you go, the more the cave draws against you.
  • Turn back while you can still carry your winnings into the light.
